After more than 25 years of coding and 20+ years in product teams, I have come up with the following cultural values I care about most. That said, as I continue to grow, this list will most likely evolve as well.

## Customer First

We're not just an engineering team. We're a product team, and we put customers first.
That means we don't just write code. We solve problems that matter to our users.
Understanding the bigger picture is crucial.

- Engineers are involved from the start, and your voice matters when deciding what features to build or tweak.
- We don't "throw code over the wall." We own the product end-to-end, from the first line of code to how it performs in the hands of users.

## Taste in Design and Execution

"Taste" isn't just about how things look. It's about how they work, how they feel, and how they meet user needs with elegance and efficiency.

- We follow design principles that prioritize user needs and experiences, just as much as we prioritize clean, effective code.
- Every feature, every interaction, and even every line of code should be approached with the user in mind.
- Whether it's a new feature, a piece of code, or a user interface, we review each other's work critically to ensure it meets our standards of taste.

## No Limits in Transparency

Our guiding principle, especially in a fully remote setting, is having no limits in transparency. Every member of the team should feel empowered by the free flow of information and the unfiltered truth.

- Our Slack workspace is a transparency hub. Discussions relevant to projects or team matters happen in open channels, so everyone is in the loop.
- We use standups to update on tasks and surface roadblocks or questions. This keeps everyone aware of what is happening across the board.
- When a key decision is made, it's documented and shared. We explain the what, why, and how.
- If project scope, deadlines, or anything else shifts, you'll hear about it right away.
- If a team member needs sick leave or any similar absence, they should inform the team promptly via Slack so everyone can plan accordingly.

## The Importance of Context in Communication

In a remote team, written words are often all we have to go on. But words without context can lead to confusion or misinterpretation. That's why we emphasize the importance of providing context in all our communications.

- If you're discussing a problem or proposing a solution in Slack, give enough background. Assume the reader doesn't have all the pieces of the puzzle you do.
- When you submit an MR, explain not just what the code does, but why it's needed and how it fits into the bigger picture.

- Include an agenda in meeting invites, or at least state the objective. This helps everyone come prepared.

## Better Than Yesterday

Here, we're all about evolution. Each line of code, every document, and all architectural choices should reflect a commitment to being "Better Than Yesterday." It's not just a tagline. It's how we work every day.

- Whether it's a small bug fix or a major feature deployment, we celebrate improvements that push us to be better than we were the day before.
- Every merge request is more than a merge. It's a learning opportunity. We review not just for errors but for improvements, always asking, "Is this better than what we had yesterday?"
- Stale documentation is a no-go. We continually update our documents to ensure they're as accurate and efficient as our code.

## Strive for Elegance

In our world, simple doesn't mean easy. It means clear, efficient, and manageable. Every line of code, every architecture choice, and every feature should be as simple as it can be.

- In code reviews, we don't just ask if something works or if it's clever. We ask, "Is this simple enough? Can it be made even simpler?"
- Before we dive into building, we document the planned architecture and features, keeping an eye out for needless complexity.
- Every team member is encouraged to question decisions. If something seems too complex, speak up.
- We revisit old solutions regularly. Even if it was the best we could do at the time, there's almost always a way to simplify further as we learn and grow.

## Beware of Accidental Complexity

As we aim for simplicity, it's crucial to identify and avoid accidental complexity - those complications that creep in without adding real value.

- Extra layers of abstraction, redundant code, or over-engineered solutions are often red flags.
- Before adding any new element to our code or architecture, we ask, "Why is this necessary?" If there's no clear answer, it's likely accidental complexity.
- When we run into issues or bugs, we dig deep to see if accidental complexity is at play. If it is, we don't just fix the issue. We simplify the system to prevent it from happening again.
